# Fireworks

The debate about fireworks is much like the debate about smoking. Strictly speaking, nobody needs fireworks, but they carry a lot of symbolic weight. Smoking was associated with freedom and with sociability. Supporters of pro-smoking causes still make those arguments today. “Smoking together is a social thing to do,” or “Being able to choose to smoke even though it harms me is a pure expression of my freedom.”

Fireworks are similar. They express freedom and joy. They have been around for about a thousand years. They began with firecrackers in China and have become a symbol of celebration and joy across cultures. Fireworks appear on countless birthday cards, and there is plenty of room for them and their relatives among our emojis. 🎉🎆

What the two have in common is that they are primarily symbols. If we ban them, nothing tangible is lost. Yet these decisions erode our culture. These days, you come out of a bar or restaurant smelling of sweat. You used to come out smelling of smoke. If fireworks are banned, drone, laser and light shows will take their place. But those are different symbols. They are a spectacle for everyone to watch, like a big fireworks display. A few wealthy or taxpayer-funded organisers put on a show for everyone else. It is like banning bicycles and telling people they can take the bus. I still get where I want to go, but travelling independently is a form of freedom. Setting off your own fireworks is an exercise in freedom, not a spectacle for elites.

If we ban small fireworks, we will soon get used to it. In five years, nobody will want them back. That is because people rarely mourn lost symbols, regardless of what those symbols were worth. People look ahead. Yet our public debate is becoming more and more collectivist. No single-family homes (we need to use space efficiently), no fireworks (we don't need them, and think of the animals), no combustion engines (because of the climate), and as much professional childcare as possible (for equality). The reasons are always sound, but the direction is worrying. The alternatives to the things we ban somehow always involve more collective provision. Apartment buildings, drone shows, smart cars, day-care centres. We seem to be growing closer as a society. At the same time, we give up more of our responsibilities and resources and hand them over to our pension funds, the state, or American and Chinese carmakers.

I am deeply sceptical of this trend. The state has few ways to exercise common sense. It makes rules, and officials and residents try to interpret them to their own advantage. A rigid set of rules takes away people's agency. Fascination and enthusiasm start with discovering something for yourself. But how are you supposed to discover anything when there are no houses left to renovate yourself because there are only apartment blocks, or when you can no longer fix your own car? You cannot put on a drone show with one drone, and you need a licence to fly it. These collectivist steps kill individual enthusiasm and the spirit of discovery. We create workers who put in 42 hours a week and are glad when the working day ends. They do what they do out of necessity, not enthusiasm. “The spark in their eyes will be gone.” And not just on New Year's Eve.
